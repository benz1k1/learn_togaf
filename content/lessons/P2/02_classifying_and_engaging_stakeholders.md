## What an engagement-planning scenario looks like

The scenario has already identified the stakeholders; now it describes their positions. The
CFO controls capital and has not been consulted. The head of operations can block the change
and understands it poorly. The regulator is interested only in one report. Two business heads
want opposite things. The question is how to plan engagement — who to involve in what, how
often, and with which deliverables — and the five-point answer classifies each stakeholder,
places them on the power/interest grid, chooses an engagement strategy per quadrant, and writes
that into the Communications Plan (TECH §3.3.2; TECH §3.3.3; ADM §3.3.11).

## Step 2 — classify positions

Develop a good understanding of the most important stakeholders and record it for refresh
during the project. The standard's example table records, per stakeholder: ability to disrupt
the change, current understanding, required understanding, current commitment, required
commitment, and required support, each rated high, medium or low (TECH §3.3.2).

Readiness to behave supportively is assessed with four questions: is the person ready to change
direction towards the target, and how ready; can they be a credible advocate or agent; how
involved do they need to be — observer or detail; and have they made a contractual commitment to
the architecture and its role in governance. For each person whose commitment is critical,
judge current and desired future commitment (TECH §3.3.2).

**Trap.** The gap between *current* and *required* understanding or commitment is the
engagement work. A scenario that says "the COO is highly committed but poorly informed" is
telling you the plan is education, not persuasion; "well informed but uncommitted" is the
reverse (TECH §3.3.2).

## Step 3 — the power/interest grid

Some stakeholders can block or advance; some are interested and some do not care. Work out
power, influence and interest so that the engagement focuses on the key individuals, and map
them onto a power/interest matrix that also indicates the strategy for engaging each of them
(TECH §3.3.3). The template stakeholder map uses three classes for the populated quadrants —
KEY PLAYERS, KEEP SATISFIED, KEEP INFORMED — and gives the artefacts each class receives
(TECH §3.4).

**Trap.** The project sponsor and programme manager appear as KEEP INFORMED in the template
map, not KEY PLAYERS: their concern is on-time, on-budget delivery of benefits, and their
artefacts are the Requirements and Principles catalogs, the Solution Concept diagram and the
Value Chain diagram. A scenario that has you flood the sponsor with platform decomposition
diagrams is planting a mismatch of artefact to class (TECH §3.4).

## Step 4 and the Communications Plan

The classification drives which catalogs, matrices and diagrams are produced and validated with
each group (TECH §3.3.4). The stakeholder map is also the starting point of the Communications
Plan, and the last step of Phase A develops that plan explicitly — where, how and when the
architects will communicate with stakeholders, including affinity groupings and communities —
before the Statement of Architecture Work is agreed with the sponsors and formally approved
under governance (ADM §3.3.2; ADM §3.3.11).

Timing matters as much as audience. The Architecture Definition should not be widely circulated
until the business transformation issues have been identified and mitigated and folded into a
"marketing" plan for the Vision; a consolidation that costs hundreds of jobs should not be
announced before an HR plan exists, and unions and shop stewards may need to be part of the
plan (TECH §7.6).

## Keep it current, and keep it honest

The analysis is updated through every phase, because different stakeholders surface in
Opportunities and Solutions, Migration Planning and Change Management (TECH §3.2). The
Practitioners' Approach adds two rules of conduct: architecture is never *sold* to a
stakeholder and preferences are never manipulated — stakeholders own the architecture and its
value priorities, and the architect acts for them while knowing when they are speaking as an
expert, as an agent, or as the architect (GUIDE:adm-practitioners §6.1.1). Where stakeholders
want incompatible things, the architect's most valuable act is facilitating the trade-off
across organisational boundaries, because stakeholders underperform when the trade-off crosses
their span of control (GUIDE:adm-practitioners §6.1.2).

The extended stakeholder map in the Practitioners' Approach records power, interest *and*
requirement per concern; a blank requirement is either a gathering gap or the stakeholder saying
"this does not matter", and knowing which is what makes trade-off possible
(GUIDE:adm-practitioners §3.3.1).

## How the 5-point answer differs from the 3-point one

| Points | What the answer does |
|---|---|
| 5 | Classifies each named stakeholder (disrupt / understanding / commitment gap), places them on the power/interest grid, assigns a strategy and tailored artefacts per quadrant, records it in the stakeholder map and the Communications Plan, and revisits it each phase |
| 3 | Uses the grid but treats it as a one-off in Phase A, or engages by seniority rather than by power and interest, or gives every quadrant the same artefacts |
| 1 | Produces a Communications Plan with no analysis behind it — a distribution list |
| 0 | Persuades the powerful stakeholder to drop their concern, or withholds the readiness issues so the Vision "lands well" |
